Course Notes
What is Globalisation?
  • Globalisation — rapid increase in the integration of global markets for goods, services, investment, and people across countries
  • Historically most trade was local; some long-distance trade existed (e.g. Silk Road) but goods were mostly produced and consumed nearby
  • Major acceleration in the 20th century, especially the last ~30 years: falling transport costs, lower tariffs, and faster communication drove the surge
  • Three key areas: trade in goods & services, labour migration, international capital flows
  • Services trade is growing — not just goods; banking, education, software are increasingly traded internationally
💡 Globalisation has NOT been a smooth downward trend in trade costs. The 1929 Great Depression triggered a sharp reversal — countries raised tariffs, trade collapsed, and it took decades to recover. Commodity market integration over 150 years has been interrupted, not linear.
Price vs Wage Convergence
  • Prices of traded goods have become more similar across countries — price convergence
  • Wages have not converged — workers doing similar jobs in rich vs poor countries still earn very differently
  • This wage gap, combined with falling trade costs, drives offshoring — firms moving production stages to lower-wage countries
Global Supply Chains & Offshoring
  • Global supply chains: modern products are made using components from many countries (phones, cars, clothing)
  • Offshoring: firms move stages of production abroad to reduce costs — enabled by falling trade and communication costs
  • Multinationals: firms that operate production across multiple countries, deepening economic integration
Why Countries Trade
Comparative Advantage
  • Opportunity cost (OC) — what you give up to produce one more unit of something
  • Comparative advantage — you have a lower OC than your trading partner for that good
  • Absolute advantage — you can produce something using fewer resources overall. Sets relative wages but does not determine who exports what
  • Trade pattern is decided by CA, not AA. A country exports whatever it produces at the lowest OC — even if it's worse at everything
💡 Even a country that is less productive at everything still has a comparative advantage in something — it will always have a lower OC in at least one good.
Economies of Scale
  • Producing more lowers cost per unit
  • Internal EoS: the firm's own size reduces its costs → leads to large firms dominating, countries trading similar products (e.g. Germany & Japan both selling cars to each other)
  • External EoS: whole industries cluster together — Silicon Valley, Hollywood, Bangalore. Shared skills, equipment, and ideas nearby
💡 Proximity-concentration trade-off: Firms want to concentrate production for scale benefits but also want to be near customers. As trade costs fall, production concentrates more.

Case Study: Vietnam & Child Labour
  • 1993–1998: Vietnam lifted rice export restrictions → rice prices rose ~30%
  • Higher household income → families kept kids in school → child labour fell by 9%
  • Nicaragua contrast: coffee price boom increased child labour — demand for farm labour rose faster than income gains helped
💡 Trade's effect on child labour depends on context — which force is stronger: the income effect or the labour demand effect?
The "Buy Local" Myth
  • Global freight ≈ 7% of greenhouse gas emissions
  • Food transport = only ~6% of food's total emissions
  • Food production itself ≈ 25% of global emissions
  • What you eat matters far more than where it comes from — switching beef → vegetables cuts emissions far more than buying local beef

💡 Gains from trade are diffuse — spread across millions of consumers as small price reductions. Losses are concentrated — specific workers and regions lose significantly. This is why trade is politically contested even when the aggregate economics support openness.
Politics of Trade
  • Economists broadly agree: free trade increases overall welfare
  • Public opinion is more sceptical — gains are unevenly distributed and some clearly lose
  • Dani Rodrik's key insight: The issue is not WHETHER trade is beneficial — it is HOW gains are distributed within society
  • Concentrated, organised losers lobby governments for protection → leads to tariffs, quotas, subsidies
  • Sanctions example: trade restrictions (e.g. on Russian exports) show how political decisions override economic logic — with mixed effects on different economies
⚠️ Limits of global governance: No strong global authority exists to enforce contracts or fairness across countries. Conflicts over who gains from globalisation occur both within and between countries.

Three Approaches to Measuring GDP
💡 GDP = Gross Domestic Product — the total value of all final goods and services produced in an economy in a given period. All three approaches should give the same answer.
ApproachHow it worksKey detail
ExpenditureAdd up all spending: C + I + G + (X−M)Household consumption is usually the largest component
Production (Value Added)Sum value added at each stage of productionAvoids double-counting intermediate inputs
IncomeSum all income earned: wages + profits + rentsCompensation of employees = largest share; relates to "labour share of income"
Value Added (Production Approach)
  • Value added = revenue from sales − cost of intermediate inputs purchased from other businesses
  • GDP counts only final goods and services — not intermediaries — to avoid double-counting
  • Example: a bakery buys flour ($1), sells bread ($3) → value added = $2
  • In Australia, mining is one of the largest contributors to GDP by industry value added
Labour Share of Income
  • Labour share of income = the portion of total national income going to wages and salaries (as opposed to profits, dividends, or rents going to owners/shareholders)
  • Compensation of employees makes up the largest share of Australian GDP by income approach

Prices usedCurrent pricesConstant base-year prices

Use forSize of economy at a point in timeTracking true growth over time
⚠️ Problem with nominal GDP: Rising prices can make GDP look higher even if production has not increased. Always use real GDP when comparing growth over time.
💡 Movie example: Recent blockbusters appear highest-grossing in nominal terms (higher ticket prices). Older films rank much higher when adjusted to real (inflation-adjusted) dollar values.
GDP per Capita & Real GDP per Capita
  • GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population → average output per person
  • Real GDP per capita = adjusts for BOTH inflation AND population → best common measure of standard of living
  • Tells us: goods/services available per person, average income, capacity for consumption and future investment
GDP Shortcomings
  • Invented by Simon Kuznets during the Great Depression to gauge economic damage
  • Inequality problem: GDP per capita is skewed by top earners — average figures do not reflect what most people actually experience
  • Excludes unpaid labour: growing your own food, raising children, volunteering — all real economic activity not counted
  • Excludes environmental damage: resource depletion, pollution, and biodiversity loss are not subtracted
⚠️ Accounting asymmetry: Clean-up spending (e.g. after the BP oil spill) adds to GDP because it is economic activity — but the environmental destruction that caused it is never subtracted. A disaster + rebuild can show rising GDP even though society is worse off.
💡 One Yale economist estimated that factoring in air pollution and climate damage could reduce measured GDP by around 2%. The official defence of GDP: it was designed to consolidate diverse economic data into one consistent measure — not to capture wellbeing.
Worldwide Income Distribution
  • Income levels range from under $1/day to over $100/day
  • Over 1 billion people live on less than $1/day; more than half the world lives on less than $10/day
  • Only ~10 million people live on more than $100/day
  • The "gap" myth: the world is NOT cleanly split into "rich" and "poor" — people exist across all income levels, with most in the middle
  • The real takeaway: no clear divide, but the difference between the very richest and very poorest is enormous
💡 Describing the world as simply "developed vs developing" is misleading. The income distribution is a continuous spread — but the extremes are vast.
Macro vs. Micro
  • Microeconomics: individual decisions by firms and households
  • Macroeconomics: the whole economy — growth, inflation, unemployment
  • Macro builds on micro — the economy is the sum of millions of individual decisions

GDP & Well-being
  • GDP per capita = total output ÷ population — rough measure of average living standards
  • Most well-being measures (health, education, life expectancy) are highly correlated with income
  • Most poverty reduction comes from growth within poor countries — not from transfers from rich countries
💡 Global average income ≈ $18/day. Ending extreme poverty (<$2.15/day) is nearly within reach. Ending poverty as defined in rich countries (<$30/day) would require a 57× increase in global GDP.
The Production Function (Solow-Swan)
Y = A × K^α × (h × L)^(1-α)
InputSymbolWhat it means
LabourLNumber of people working
Human CapitalHSkills and education of workers
Physical CapitalKMachines, buildings, tools
ProductivityATechnology & organisation — no diminishing returns
Two Key Properties
  • Constant returns to scale: double all inputs → output exactly doubles. Build an identical factory next door → identical output
  • Diminishing returns to capital: add more machines while keeping workers constant → each new machine adds less and less. The 100th machine adds less than the 1st
💡 Diminishing returns mean you can't grow forever by buying more machines. Eventually adding more capital stops being worth it — growth stalls unless something else changes.

💡 Capital and human capital drive "catch-up growth." Long-run sustained growth requires ongoing improvements in productivity — i.e. technological progress.
Romer Model — Ideas & Technology
  • Technology comes from new ideas
  • Ideas are non-rival — one person using an idea doesn't stop another from using it (unlike a machine)
  • This creates a positive externality — one person creates an idea, everyone benefits
  • "Standing on the shoulders of giants" — each new idea makes the next one easier to find
💡 Because ideas are non-rival, technology can keep improving indefinitely — unlike physical capital which faces diminishing returns.
Policy Lessons for Growth
  • Property rights → people invest more when assets are protected
  • Political stability → instability kills investment (e.g. Argentina: 6 military coups 1930–1976)
  • Efficient regulation → misallocation of resources (putting capital and labour in wrong places) explains much of the rich-poor gap
  • IP laws & R&D subsidies → encourage innovation by making it profitable
Growth & the Environment
  • Production function doesn't account for resource depletion or pollution — these are negative externalities
  • Climate change = global externality — each country only gets a small share of the benefit from cutting its own emissions → free-rider problem → no country has enough incentive to act alone
  • Solution requires multinational cooperation (e.g. Montreal Protocol for ozone, WTO for trade disputes)
💡 "Climate change economics and technological change are manifestations of the same fundamental phenomenon — a global externality."

Two Views of the Economy
  • Neoclassical: prices adjust quickly — demand changes → prices move → balance restored. Government spending doesn't change real output, only technology does
  • Keynesian: prices are sticky — when demand rises, businesses produce more instead of just raising prices. Governments can boost real output
💡 Week 8 uses the Keynesian approach — sticky prices mean demand shocks change output and employment in the short run.
Consumer Spending & MPC
C = C̄ + a × (Y − T)
  • MPC (a) = fraction of each extra $1 of income that households spend. Always between 0 and 1
  • Example: MPC = 0.8 → for every $1 earned, 80c spent, 20c saved
  • Consumption smoothers: plan spending based on lifetime income, save windfalls, borrow in bad times → low effective MPC
  • Hand-to-mouth households: can't borrow, spend every dollar earned now → MPC ≈ 1, common among low-income households
The Expenditure Multiplier
Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC)
  • When government spends $1 more, total GDP rises by more than $1 — spending becomes income → spent again → chain reaction
  • Example: MPC = 0.5 → Multiplier = 2 → $10b spending = $20b increase in GDP
  • Higher MPC = bigger multiplier = larger ripple effect
💡 The multiplier is the key mechanism behind fiscal stimulus — a small increase in government spending can have a large effect on total output.
Fiscal Policy
  • Government using spending (G) and taxes (T) to stabilise the economy
  • Automatic stabilisers: work without any new decisions — in a recession, unemployment insurance pays out automatically and tax revenue falls automatically
  • Discretionary policy: deliberate government decisions, e.g. Australia's COVID stimulus — $90b JobKeeper + $20b Coronavirus Supplement = $120b (~7% of GDP)
⚠️ Problems with fiscal policy: (1) Time lags — slow to notice a problem, pass legislation and see effects; (2) Funding — needs higher taxes or borrowing (can crowd out private investment, may be unsustainable long-run)
Money
  • Three functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value
  • Commodity money: has intrinsic value (gold, silver, salt)
  • Fiat money: no intrinsic value — backed by government. All modern money is fiat

Inflation
  • Caused by too much money chasing too few goods — money growth drives inflation long-run
  • Headline inflation = % change in CPI
  • Underlying inflation = excludes volatile items (e.g. weather-affected food) — cleaner trend
  • Expected inflation costs: menu costs (reprinting prices), shoe-leather costs (more bank trips), complicated contracts
  • Unexpected inflation costs: hard to plan, distorts relative prices, discourages investment, redistributes wealth
💡 Redistributive effect: unexpected inflation hurts lenders (retirees, banks) and helps borrowers (homeowners, govt) — debts repaid in dollars worth less than expected.
💡 Phillips curve: when the economy runs above potential (positive output gap), inflation tends to be higher than expected. When weak, inflation tends to be lower.
Monetary Policy — The RBA
  • Three legal goals (Reserve Bank Act 1959): currency stability, full employment, economic welfare
  • Main tool: the cash rate = interest rate on overnight loans between banks — the benchmark rate for the whole economy
To stimulate (↓ rates):
  • RBA buys government bonds → injects cash into banks → cash rate falls → borrowing cheaper → C and I rise → GDP rises
To cool (↑ rates):
  • RBA sells government bonds → removes cash → cash rate rises → borrowing expensive → spending falls → GDP falls
Fisher equation: i = r + πe  (nominal rate = real rate + expected inflation)
  • Lower rates affect the economy through: cheaper borrowing, higher asset prices, weaker dollar (boosts exports), lower government debt costs
⚠️ Why policy is hard: Blunt instruments (few tools, many goals) · Data lags (don't know what's happening until weeks later) · Hard to measure potential output · Zero lower bound (can't cut nominal rates below ~0%, so monetary policy runs out of ammunition in deep recessions)
